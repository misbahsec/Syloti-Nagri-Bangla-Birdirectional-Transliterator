    About

    Sylheti, and the script made for it

    ꠍꠤꠟꠐꠤ

    The language

    Sylheti is an Eastern Indo-Aryan language spoken by around 11 million people, primarily in the Sylhet Division of Bangladesh, the Barak Valley of Assam, and northern Tripura. Large Sylheti-speaking communities also live in the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, and the Middle East.

    Sylheti is closely related to Bengali, and many speakers use both languages. However, Sylheti has its own distinct phonology, vocabulary, grammar, and verb forms, giving it a linguistic identity of its own. It also has a rich historical writing tradition, including the Syloti Nagri script, a distinct script historically used to write the language. Sylheti has a long and unique linguistic and cultural history and is an important part of the heritage of the Sylheti-speaking community.

    UNESCO classifies Sylheti as a vulnerable language, highlighting the importance of documenting, preserving, and promoting it for future generations.

    ꠍꠤꠟꠐꠤ ꠘꠣꠉꠞꠤ

    The script

    Sylheti has its own alphabet, Syloti Nagri. No one knows exactly when it appeared. Tradition ties it to the time of Shah Jalal in the early 14th century; the oldest known manuscript is dated either 1559 or 1774, depending on which calendar the date follows.

    With only 5 vowels and 28 consonants it was quick to learn, and for centuries people used it to copy and read puthi, the long religious and story poems of the region. Around 1870 Moulvi Abdul Karim made woodblock type for it and opened the Islamia Press in Sylhet town, and more presses followed in Sunamganj, Shillong and Calcutta. Printing faded after Partition, and the Islamia Press is reported to have burned during the Liberation War in 1971. Computer fonts arrived in the late 1990s, and Unicode added the script in 2005.

    Why Syltrans

    Today most people who write Sylheti use Bengali or English letters, and few can read Nagri. That leaves old books closed to most readers, and it leaves the script out of everyday writing.

    Syltrans moves text between the two scripts. It changes letters, not words: the same sentence goes in and comes out. Type in Bengali letters, English letters or a mix to see it in Nagri, or paste Nagri to read it in Bengali letters. The conversion follows fixed rules and a small word list, so check names and rare words before you publish.
